The Great Emu War of 1932

Background

In late 1932, farmers in the Campion district of Western Australia faced a serious agricultural problem: a population of roughly 20,000 emus had migrated inland after their breeding season, drawn by the region's newly cleared farmland and its water sources. The emus trampled fences, opening paths for rabbits to invade cropland, and ate or ruined wheat crops that had already suffered from the economic pressures of the Great Depression. Many of the farmers involved were veterans of the First World War who had been settled on this land under a soldier-settlement scheme, and they petitioned the Australian government for military assistance.

The Military Response

The Minister of Defence at the time, Sir George Pearce, approved the deployment of soldiers armed with two Lewis automatic machine guns, along with 10,000 rounds of ammunition. The operation was led by Major G.P.W. Meredith of the Seventh Heavy Battery of the Royal Australian Artillery. The plan was straightforward on paper: use machine-gun fire to cull the emu population and protect the farmland. Also present, unofficially, was a cinematographer, who was sent to film the operation for propaganda-style newsreel purposes.

The operation began on 2 November 1932 near the town of Campion. Soldiers initially attempted to herd emus toward the waiting guns, but the birds quickly scattered into small groups, making them difficult targets. In the first encounter, the machine gunners opened fire at long range on a flock of around fifty emus, but only a handful were killed before the gun jammed and the rest of the birds fled.

Why the Emus Were Difficult to Defeat

Emus proved far more resilient and tactically evasive than the military had anticipated. They could run at speeds up to roughly 50 kilometres per hour, allowing them to outpace pursuing vehicles across rough terrain. They tended to scatter in different directions when threatened rather than staying in a group, which frustrated attempts at a coordinated ambush. Major Meredith later remarked, in comments widely quoted afterward, that the emus seemed to employ guerrilla tactics, splitting into small units that were hard to target effectively, and that they could "face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks."

A notable failed attempt on 4 November involved soldiers hiding near a dam that emus were known to frequent. When a large group of birds approached, the gunners opened fire, but the gun jammed again after only a small number of birds were hit, and the rest escaped.

Escalation and Withdrawal

By 8 November 1932, after roughly a week of operations, only a modest number of emus had been confirmed killed relative to the ammunition expended — estimates from military reports at the time suggested somewhere between 50 and a few hundred birds killed for thousands of rounds fired, though exact figures were disputed. Due to the operation's poor results and mounting public criticism, the military was withdrawn.

Farmers continued to request assistance, and a second, smaller deployment was authorized in early-to-mid November 1932. This second phase reported a higher rate of success, with Major Meredith's final report claiming 986 confirmed kills and suggesting several hundred more emus later died from wounds sustained during the operation, out of roughly 9,860 rounds fired — meaning it was later estimated that it took about ten rounds of ammunition per confirmed emu killed. The operation formally ended in December 1932.

Aftermath

The Great Emu War, as it came to be known in the press both in Australia and internationally, was widely regarded as an embarrassing failure for the military and was mocked in newspapers of the time. Ornithologist Dominic Serventy later described the operation as an attempted "massacre" that had largely turned into a fiasco for the humans involved, given how outmatched the soldiers were by the emus' speed and dispersal tactics.

Despite the campaign's failure to significantly reduce the emu population, farmers continued to lobby for support. Rather than further military intervention, the government subsequently reinstated a bounty system, paying farmers for emu culling, which proved considerably more effective and cost-efficient than the machine-gun operation had been. A barrier fencing system was also expanded over subsequent years to help keep emus and other wildlife away from cultivated land.

Legacy

The Great Emu War has since become a well-known piece of Australian folklore, frequently referenced in popular culture as an example of an ill-conceived military campaign and as a lighthearted symbol of nature "winning" against human technology. It is often cited humorously as one of the few instances in which a national military force is popularly considered to have been defeated by wildlife. The Australian War Memorial and various historians have used the episode to illustrate both the limitations of machine guns against dispersed, fast-moving animal targets and the political pressures that can drive unconventional uses of military resources during periods of agricultural and economic hardship.
